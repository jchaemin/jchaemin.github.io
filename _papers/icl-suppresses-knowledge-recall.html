In-Context Examples Suppress Scientific Knowledge Recall in LLMs

COLM'26: Conference on Language Modeling. 2026.

Chaemin Jang, Woojin Park, Hyeok Yun, Dongman Lee, Jihee Kim

KAIST  ·  Shanghai Jiao Tong University

  arXiv Back to Publications

Abstract

Scientific problem solving often depends on recovering structure that isn't directly visible — a reaction constant, a demand elasticity, a governing law behind a table of numbers. Large language models can often name and apply the right formula from memory alone. We show that this ability is easy to knock loose: adding in-context examples shifts the model away from that memorized, knowledge-driven derivation and toward fitting the pattern in front of it — even when the examples were generated by the same formula the model already knew, and so add no genuinely new information.

We call this knowledge displacement, and trace it across 60 latent-structure-recovery tasks spanning five scientific domains, 6,000 trials, and four models. The direction of the shift is consistent everywhere we look. Its effect on accuracy is not: depending on how the pattern-fitting strategy that moves in compares to the recall it replaces, the same displacement can help, hurt, or leave accuracy roughly unchanged. Either way, the model has stopped reasoning from what it knows.

The Strategy Shift

Headline accuracy alone makes the five domains look like they tell different stories — hurt here, apparently helped there, unchanged elsewhere. But classify what strategy the model is actually using in each trial — knowledge-driven derivation ("Parametric") versus fitting a pattern from the examples ("Empirical" or "Hybrid") — and every domain tells the same story: 10 in-context examples pull the model away from derivation and toward pattern-fitting, by a similar margin, whether or not that switch happens to help.

Domain0-shot Parametric10-shot ParametricShift (pp)Accuracy Δ
Economics100.0%80.3%−19.7−15.7
Geoscience85.7%65.7%−20.0+13.3
Biology87.8%74.7%−13.1+1.3
Chemistry91.8%81.3%−10.5−6.2
Physics99.8%93.8%−6.0+0.2

Share of trials classified as knowledge-driven ("Parametric") by an automated CoT classifier, 98% agreement with human annotators on a 100-trace validation set. Every domain loses 6–20 points of parametric reasoning — the shift is uniform even where the accuracy effect (right column) points in opposite directions.

The size of the shift bears no relationship to its effect on accuracy. Economics loses 19.7 points of parametric reasoning and ends up 15.7 points worse. Geoscience loses a nearly identical 20.0 points and appears 13.3 points better. Same mechanism, opposite headline outcome — because what determines the accuracy consequence isn't whether the shift happens, but how the strategy that replaces derivation happens to compare against it in that domain.

A Causal Check: The Logit Gap

The strategy-shift numbers above come from classifying chain-of-thought text, and reviewers pushed on an obvious concern during review: CoT isn't guaranteed to faithfully reflect what a model is actually computing. To test the claim at the level of the model's own output distribution instead of its stated reasoning, a paired log-probability check was added during the rebuttal on three open-weight models — Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3. For each input, it compares log-probability of two candidate answers, zero-shot vs. 10-shot: the example-fit answer (yemp, from fitting the 10 demonstrations) and the knowledge-derived answer (yknow, from the correct formula).

ModelΔℓ(y_emp)Δℓ(y_know)Paired gap% instances shifted to y_emp
Qwen2.5-7B+1.37−1.70+3.0776.7%
Llama-3.1-8B+2.82−1.39+4.2189.3%
Mistral-7B-v0.3+2.77−0.89+3.6682.7%

Δℓ = log P(answer | x, 10-shot) − log P(answer | x, 0-shot), same 300 instances per model. Added during the COLM review discussion in response to reviewers pressing on causality; slated for the camera-ready as part of Appendix L.

The reason this matters: if examples only added a new option without touching the old one — augmentation rather than displacement — Δℓ(yknow) would sit near zero. It doesn't. On the same input, probability mass moves toward the example-fit answer and away from the knowledge-derived answer at the same time, by 3–4 nats combined. That paired, bidirectional pattern is what distinguishes genuine displacement from a model simply picking up an extra option — direct evidence, independent of the CoT classifier, that in-context examples are actively pulling probability away from what the model's own knowledge would have produced.

How a Task Looks

Each task gives the model raw observables and asks it to recover a hidden parameter, then compute a target through the domain's governing law — not just map inputs to outputs. In the 10-shot condition, the 10 worked examples are generated by that same law with independently sampled numbers, so they're fully consistent with, and add nothing beyond, what the model would already need to know. Below is one of the paper's actual traces: a Cournot duopoly task from Economics, where zero-shot gets it right and 10-shot doesn't.

Zero-shot — Parametric, correct
Step 1: Identify Cournot model
  "Two firms, linear demand P = a − bQ"

Step 2: Derive equilibrium
  q_i* = (a−c)/3b,  Q* = 2(a−c)/3b

Step 3: Compute CS from the formula
  CS = ½(a−P*)Q* = 2(a−c)²/9b
     = 1481.5  ✓

derives consumer surplus from the Cournot equilibrium

10-shot — Hybrid, wrong
Step 1: Fit a pattern from the examples
  "CS scales roughly as 0.5 × Q² / slope"

Step 2: Apply the fitted rule
  CS ≈ 0.5 × 66.7² / 1.5
     = 1482.1 ... or 1507.3  ✗

fits a numerical heuristic that's inconsistent across trials

Given nothing but the question, the model correctly re-derives the answer. Given 10 examples generated by that very equilibrium formula, it abandons the derivation for a rough numerical rule that lands on a different wrong number each time — even though the zero-shot answer (1481.5) was already correct.

Why Knowledge Recall Is So Easy to Suppress

One follow-up experiment: keep every number and equation in a task identical, but replace the domain vocabulary with meaningless pseudonyms — "price" becomes "Alpha_Wave," "consumer surplus" becomes "Gamma_Rad." Zero-shot accuracy collapses from a 55–88% range down to 3–14%, even though the underlying math hasn't changed at all. The model isn't deriving the relationship from the numbers in front of it — it's recognizing which formula to use from the words describing the problem. Add the 10 examples back in under the same pseudonyms, and accuracy partially recovers to 22–40%, since pattern-fitting over numeric pairs doesn't need the vocabulary in the first place. That's part of why a few worked examples are enough to knock recall off course: the strategy they displace was never doing much reasoning to begin with.

What We Find

The shift is uniform; the outcome isn't. Adding 10 examples pulls 6–20 percentage points of trials away from knowledge-driven derivation in every domain — including Physics and Biology, where headline accuracy barely moves. The model doesn't selectively drop recall where it would be wrong; it drops it everywhere, and aggregate accuracy alone hides that.

A strong prior gets worse; a weak one looks better. In Economics, zero-shot derivation is right 88.3% of the time — losing it to examples costs 15.7 points. In Geoscience, zero-shot derivation is right only 55.3% of the time, largely because the model defaults to textbook constants instead of the values in front of it — so losing it to pattern-fitting looks like a 13.3-point gain, even though pure pattern-fitting accuracy on its own actually drops from 64.6% to 54.4% across the same shift.

Recall is trigger-based, not structural. Swapping domain vocabulary for meaningless pseudonyms — leaving the math untouched — collapses zero-shot accuracy to single digits. The model is recognizing keywords, not re-deriving the relationship from the numbers, which is a large part of why a few worked examples are enough to knock it off its recalled formula.
